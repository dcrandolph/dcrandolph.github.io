I watched the sunrise this morning, as I do on most mornings. I highly recommend it; it's a beautiful way to start the day.

Actually, that's not completely true. I do wake up while it's still dark outside. My habitual nature and my ever-growing puppy's ever-growing proclivity for breakfast ensure that. I do also encourage you to do the same. That's not the falsehood.

I've never seen the Sun rise. Not once in my life. I've never seen it set, for that matter. And neither have you. Nobody has.

The Sun is a fixed point, for all intents and purposes. It never sets, nor does it rise. It just stays put. *We* move.

Of course you already know that; aside from a few social media goofballs, most folks can agree on the concept of a heliocentric galaxy. It's common knowledge.

Isn't it funny that something so simple is so hard to express in our language? It's pretty easy to say "I watched the sunrise this morning," but the more accurate phrasing of "This morning I observed that the Earth's rotation brought me back around to a place where my surroundings were once again illuminated by the Sun's rays" is a little bit cumbersome. As far as I know, "earthrise" is not a word.

It's worth taking a minute to think about bias. As we've seen, our language is biased toward the idea that we're at the center of the galaxy. Are there any other areas of life where we're biased in favor of narcissism? Food for thought.

As far as I'm concerned, the Sun can keep the job. Being the center of the galaxy can't be all it's cracked up to be. If you're the end-all be-all, or even if you just *think* you are, it's tricky not to see the rest of existence on your terms, instead of letting things be as they are. We used to think that we were at the middle of everything. When we saw that big orb in the sky, we assumed it had human faults and foibles. We made it fickle. We prayed to it, beseeched it to stick around, tried to appease it. None of that worked, not because the Sun was egotistical, but because *we* were. As we learned about our galaxy, we realized that we weren't the center. Along the way we learned that the Sun just shines, just gives light and heat and life, all the time. We turn away sometimes, but that's not the Sun's doing.

What a silly thing, to pretend that the source of so much good was subject to the same fears of death and darkness that drive so much negative, selfish, unnecessary human behavior. I'm glad we've risen to a level where that kind of pretense is no longer comforting.

You know where I'm going with this. I once had a conspiracy theorist tell me, after a glass of wine, that they *needed* to believe in sinister plots and dark agendas because it made them feel better about themselves and the dark parts of their own experience. Lots of people believe in an angry, vengeful God because they themselves are wrestling with those same traits.

We really do walk by faith and not by sight. Your belief determines your experience; no surprise there. So there's a bit of a vicious cycle: If I feel egotistical, hurt, afraid, angry, and so on, I can choose to project those feelings onto my life. Doing so will give me plenty of experiences to justify and intensify those feelings. On and on. This is why Facebook looks the way that it does.

But we know the way out. When we realized that we weren't at the center of the solar system, we learned that something bigger than us exists. That idea set us free from some pretty goofy old ideas. Same thing here.

Conspiracy theories are just a fancy way of taking one's ego out for a walk. Same with "sinners in the hands of an angry God" theology. The trick is to find, and believe in, and celebrate something bigger than ourselves, something that gives light and life. Because that's what's happening.

Once we get out of the way, we realize that the Sun just shines. God just loves. The universe is a good place, even if we don't always feel good. And *that* knowing is the beginning of a lot of healing.
