Gabriel stole gas. Definitely. He was our Youth Sponsor, in the days before background checks would have prevented him from being one, and we were on the way back from a retreat in the middle of nowhere. A little gas station in a one-stoplight town somewhere not too far removed from swampland. Gabe insisted we stay in the rental van. We saw him pump the gas, in the days before you had to put a credit card in first. Maybe they instituted that because of him. We saw him run back to the drivers seat and slam the door. We heard him peel out. We felt the sudden g-force of innocence blasted away. We were church kids. We'd never seen an adult do anything like that, except in movies that we were not supposed to watch.

It was the coolest thing we'd ever seen.

But he was always doing things like that. We loved him for it. In a time of life where it seemed as though the key to becoming a man, a dad, a husband was to emit a fog of bullshit and believe it, to get high on one's own supply of lies, to fill every nook and cranny with bluster and peel out before it could be challenged, we thought for sure Gabe was raw, uncut, pure.

Gabriel told us, at 92 miles an hour, that you could defeat the governor on a rental van by putting your foot to the floor until you heard the revs max out, then let up, then do it again, and after a while you would get some play in the mechanism and could go as fast as you wanted. We were 14. I think that technique worked for him in general, as a man who found wriggle room in everything he did, like Houdini squirming out of a straitjacket.

All the other adults in our lives had sold out, straightened up, given up. All they wanted to do was get us to do the same. Zombies making zombies. But not Gabe. Gabe represented another way. Gabe did not have a job that anybody could discern. Gabe wore a brown polyester suit, cowboy cut, in 1989, and it worked. Gabe had a beard when nobody else did, and said "far out" after everybody else did not. 

And he definitely stole gas that one time.

One time, when it was the youth group's turn to serve coffee after church, which amounted to cutting up day-old pastries and putting them on dusty napkins, and refilling the ancient greasy coffee urn about a hundred times, and then standing around, I saw Gabe across Fellowship Hall, talking to an octogenarian member of the Ladies' Auxiliary. She looked angry. At him. Maybe at us, too. But Gabriel just smiled at her and kept smiling. He had a way of looking without blinking. We wondered if he could hypnotize people. He told us he could, one time. 

And then I noticed his foot. He was moving the toe of his left boot up and down, heel on the ground, like he was playing an invisible bass drum, the same one he played whenever Taking Care of Business, the song I associate with him still, came on. He hit the imaginary pedal over and over again, while the angry lady talked at him. No Bachman–Turner Overdrive 4/4 tempo, though. Sometimes he tapped, sometimes he really leaned in.

Later on, after most folks had gone to Church Lunch and Gabe was watching but not helping us clean up pastry crumbs and lipstick laden coffee cups, I asked him what he was doing. 

"That old lady was mad for some reason. Don't know why. But she was trying to lay all that on me, and I got too much good stuff to do. You know those garbage cans where you hit the pedal and the lid comes up? That's where I put all her crap. You don't have to pick up what other people lay down, kid. Remember that."

I never forgot, though sometimes I have trouble believing. But maybe Gabe was right. Sometimes the right answer is to put your foot down and smile and peel out.
