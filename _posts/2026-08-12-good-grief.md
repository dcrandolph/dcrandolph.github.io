**Thoughts are things,** or so I was told, over and over again, growing up. I'm from a religious tradition that embraced the power of positive thinking and, it could be argued, paved the way for a lot of the motivational, self-help, and pop psychology we see today. I was taught that changing one's life was simply a matter of changing one's mind. 

Sounds good, right? I thought so, too. I spent many years repeating affirmations and working on my mindset.

But I _also_ spent a lot of time blaming myself when things didn't go well. It's easy to distort the idealist concept into a kind of victim-blaming. Got a flat tire? Stubbed your toe? Lost your job? If life is mindset, if your ideas determine your experience, what's in _your_ crummy brain that's causing those bad things?

Consciousness is important; it _does_ mediate and mitigate and uplift what we experience. But consciousness doesn't live between our ears. When you know that you're in love, for example, _where_ exactly do you know it? In your head, or in your heart? Where is the knowing when you know that you're alive, or inspired, or even sad?

It's important to get out of our heads and into our hearts. I don't want to sound anti-intellectual, but I do want us to stop thinking that life begins and ends in our skulls.

Lots of people have a really solid intellectual grasp of their worthiness, but don't feel that in their hearts. As a result, every touchy-feely affirmation about how great they are feels like a lie. As a result of _that,_ their experience only gets worse.

If you blame yourself and your ideas for any bad times, you might feel a desire to keep that blame to yourself. I've seen people show up at my old church hurting, bruised emotionally and sometimes even physically, but pretending that all is well. I've done that too.

Somewhere along the way faith has been confused with self-deception.

Let's fix that. Let's learn how to feel our feelings so that we can learn from them. **I want you to know that it's okay to grieve.**

You and I have experienced loss. We can either acknowledge that, face it, feel our way through it, and learn from it, or we can pretend it never happened. One of those guarantees growth. The other one insures repetition.

This part of you that feels, this part of you that _knows,_ is the part that determines your next experience. Let's lean in. Let's lean in, even when the feelings seem negative. The part of you that grieves is also the part of you that has, by definition, remained after something else has gone away. You can learn from that if you honor it.
